Recently proposed variational signal decomposition methods like adaptive chirp mode decom­
position (ACMD) and generalized dispersive mode decomposition (GDMD) have attracted much
attention in various fields. However, these methods are difficult to simultaneously separate chirp
components and dispersive components. This paper proposes a variational generalized nonlinear
mode decomposition (VGNMD) framework to address this issue. The VGNMD first introduces an
adaptive time–frequency fusion and clustering (ATFFC) scheme to improve noise robustness and
resolution of time–frequency distribution (TFD) of signal in a noisy environment and to accu­
rately obtain the TFD of each mode. Then, a mode-type discrimination criterion is established to
categorize modes into chirp modes or dispersive modes based on their time–frequency (TF)
ridges. Finally, with these TF ridges as initial instantaneous frequencies (IFs) or initial group
delays (GDs), a variational optimization algorithm is applied to accurately reconstruct the modes
and refine their IFs or GDs. Simulated examples and real-life applications to bat echolocation
signal analysis and railway wheel/rail fault diagnosis are considered to show the effectiveness of
the VGNMD. The results indicate that the proposed approach can accurately extract chirp modes
and dispersive modes simultaneously, and is well-suitable for analyzing nonlinear signals with
discontinuous TF patterns.

1. Introduction
Condition monitoring and fault diagnosis of mechanical equipment is of great significance to ensure their safe and stable operation
[1–5]. Signal decomposition can suppress the environmental noise and interference components in the mechanical vibration signal by
decomposing the signal into a series of sub-signal components with clear physical meaning [6,7]. With the development of fault
diagnosis in mechanical equipment towards refinement, signal decomposition plays an increasingly important role in weak fault
diagnosis and quantitative identification [8]. However, for large mechanical systems such as rail vehicles, the complex operating
environment (multi-source excitation, time-varying conditions, etc.) makes the condition monitoring signals often show the charac­
teristics of multiple modes mixing, complex non-stationary modulation, and strong noise interference [9,10]. Specifically, due to the
coupling effect of parts in the mechanical system, the vibration signal of one part may contain multiple components from multiple
parts, and may even have both harmonic-like (i.e., chirp mode with instantaneous frequency (IF) varying with time [11]) and pulselike components (i.e., dispersive mode with group delay (GD) varying with frequency [12]). It is a very challenging and practical work
to accurately separate these modes and extract their IFs and GDs [13,14].

Most of the existing signal decomposition methods are mainly devoted to the separation of a certain class of components (i.e., chirp
modes or dispersive modes). Chirp modes generally carry important information about the type and degree of machine fault [15].
Many decomposition methods, such as empirical mode decomposition (EMD) [16], local mean decomposition [17], empirical wavelet
transform [18] and variational mode decomposition (VMD) [19], are used to separate chirp modes. These methods have aroused much
attention in various fields because of their distinct benefits, such as the high adaptability (e.g., EMD) [20] and the strong noise
robustness (e.g., VMD) [21]. However, most of these above methods are restricted to decomposing narrow-band modes that are well
separated in the frequency domain [22]. To effectively analyze wideband non-stationary signals, variational nonlinear chirp mode
decomposition (VNCMD) is proposed recently based on an optimal demodulation algorithm [23]. Different from the frequency domain
filter bank of VMD [24], the VNCMD is essentially a time–frequency (TF) domain filter bank with the center frequencies being the
estimated IFs, which can decompose chirp signals with very close or even crossed modes. Furthermore, the adaptive chirp mode
decomposition (ACMD) [25] and data-driven ACMD (DD-ACMD) [26] are proposed to enhance the adaptability of the VNCMD. Note
that although the above variational signal decomposition methods gradually meet both high adaptability and good noise robustness in
analyzing non-stationary signals, they are inapplicable for dispersive signal analysis because the chirp mode model is unable to
characterize the dispersion effect.
Dispersive modes are often employed to locate sources and identify systems. For dispersive signal decomposition, the dispersion
compensation method (DCM) can effectively reduce the dispersion effect and has aroused much interest [27]. The method compresses
the dispersive waveform with a frequency-varying GD into a temporal pulse with a constant GD, which can be extracted by rectangular
time windows. Motivated by the DCM and ACMD, a generalized dispersive mode decomposition (GDMD) method is developed to deal
with overlapped or crossed dispersive modes [28]. In this method, the mode decomposition issue is formulated as an optimal
dispersion compensation problem, which is solved by a joint-estimation algorithm. In essence, the optimal dispersion compensation is
a frequency-domain dual version of the optimal demodulation algorithm in VNCMD and ACMD. Due to its good decomposition ability
and noise robustness, the GDMD is widely used in structural health monitoring and multi-channel signal decomposition [29]. It is
worth noting that these variational signal decomposition methods extract signal modes through a variational optimization algorithm,
which needs to be driven by initial IFs or initial GDs. Specifically, the ACMD obtains initial IFs by Hilbert transform to drive the
variational optimization algorithm to extract chirp modes in time domain. The GDMD obtains initial GDs by extracting TF ridges to
drive the variational optimization algorithm to extract dispersive modes in frequency domain. Nevertheless, limited by the dispersive
mode model, these methods are difficult to simultaneously extract dispersive modes and chirp modes. In addition, real-life signals may
have certain discontinuous components that only exist for a limited duration and frequency bands in a reference interval. Most of the
above methods define the mode model over the entire reference interval, and thus they cannot extract these discontinuous
components.
Different from the above signal decomposition method, time–frequency analysis (TFA) can simultaneously characterize chirp
features and dispersion features in the TF domain. However, limited by the Heisenberg-Gabor inequality, the time–frequency distri­
butions (TFDs) obtained by traditional TFA methods are usually subjected to poor energy concentration. To improve the resolution of
the TFD, researchers developed many post-processing methods based on TF reassignment like synchrosqueezing transform (SST) [30]
and its improved version [31–33]. Unfortunately, the above methods only move the TF coefficients along one direction to maintain
signal reconstruction capability, and thus can not improve the time resolution and frequency resolution simultaneously. To address
these issues, time–frequency-multisqueezing transform (TFMST) [34] is proposed based on a “divide-and-conquer” strategy, which can
not only effectively characterize the mixture signals of the harmonic-like and pulse-like components but also has the capacity of signal
reconstruction. In addition, TF fusion is also an effective technology to improve the TF resolution by fusing TFDs with different pa­
rameters [35–37]. It is worth noting that most of the above methods focus on obtaining high-resolution TFD and do not give an
effective strategy for separating chirp components and dispersive components simultaneously.
As discussed before, it is a challenging work to simultaneously extract chirp modes and dispersive modes in a nonlinear signal.
Inspired by TF fusion technology and variational signal decomposition methods of ACMD and GDMD, a variational generalized
nonlinear mode decomposition (VGNMD) framework is proposed to adaptively classify modes and further accurately extract these
modes and their IFs or GDs. Different from ACMD and GDMD, the proposed VGNMD can adaptively obtain the type of each mode and
its time–frequency ridge, which are used as the initial parameters to drive the variational optimization algorithm to simultaneously
extract chirp modes and dispersive modes from the complex non-stationary signal. The main contributions of this paper can be
summarized as follows. Firstly, an adaptive TF fusion and clustering (ATFFC) scheme is proposed to improve the TF resolution and
eliminate noise interference, and further obtain high-quality TFD of each mode. Then, a mode-type discrimination criterion is
introduced to classify modes into chirp modes or dispersive modes based on TF ridges of modes. Meanwhile, the support intervals of
these modes are obtained by the nonzero values of TF ridges. Finally, driven by these TF ridges, the variational optimization algorithm
accurately extracts modes and their IFs or GDs. Further, a high-resolution TFD is reconstructed with these modes and IFs or GDs.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we introduce the generalized nonlinear signal model and review the
variational optimization algorithm in ACMD and GDMD. The proposed VGNMD framework is detailed in Section III. In Section IV,
some simulated and practical signals are provided to test the performance of the VGNMD. We conclude our paper in Section V.

variational optimization algorithm has good decomposition ability and noise robustness, neither ACMD nor GDMD can effectively
analyze the GNSs since they can not determine the types and support intervals of modes.
3. Variational generalized nonlinear mode decomposition
In this section, we present a unified variational generalized nonlinear mode decomposition (VGNMD) framework to achieve the
simultaneous extraction of chirp modes and dispersive modes in the GNS. The framework first introduces an ATFFC scheme to obtain
the high-quality TFDs of modes and then classifies modes based on their TF ridges by a mode-type discrimination criterion. Finally,
according to the types of modes and their TF ridges, the variational optimization algorithm is driven to extract modes within their
respective support intervals.

3.2. Mode-type discrimination criterion
In this subsection, we introduce a mode-type discrimination criterion to classify mode according to the TFDs of modes obtained in
the previous subsection. The idea of the discrimination criterion is that the chirp mode is a signal with nonlinear time-varying IF and its
time–frequency ridge is a single-valued function of time. The dispersive mode is a signal with nonlinear frequency-varying GD and its
time–frequency ridge is a single-valued function of frequency. In other words, if the TF ridge is a multi-valued function of time, it is a
dispersive mode, and if the TF ridge of a mode is a multi-valued function of frequency, it is a chirp mode. To sum up, the discrimination
problem of mode type can be transformed into the identification problem of the function type corresponding to TF ridge. Enlight­
eningly, the function type of a TF ridge can be identified according to the number of ridge points corresponding to the coordinates.
Specifically, if there are multiple points on a TF ridge with the same frequency coordinate, the TF ridge corresponds to the multi-valued
function of the frequency and the corresponding signal mode is a chirp mode (Ridge2 in Fig. 3). If there are multiple points on a TF
ridge with the same time coordinate, the TF ridge corresponds to the multi-valued function of the time and the corresponding signal
mode a dispersive mode (Ridge3 in Fig. 3). Note that if each point of a TF ridge uniquely corresponds to the coordinate in both di­
rections (i.e., time and frequency) (Ridge1 in Fig. 3), the above idea is not applicable and an effective idea is to compare the slope of the
ridge (SR) and the slope of 45-degree line (i.e., constant 1). To implement the above idea, an average distance index (ADI) is introduced

direction (i.e., time or frequency) and p = 1, ⋯, P is their sequence number (see Fig. 3); Ve (p) represents the sequence number of time
sampling corresponding to the p-th ridge point under frequency coordinate e (such as Ve (1) and Ve (2) in Fig. 3); Ve (0) = Ve (1) − 1;
Vg (p) represents the sequence number of frequency sampling corresponding to the p-th point ridge under time coordinate g (such as
Vg (1) and Vg (2) in Fig. 3); Vg (0) = Vg (1) − 1. According to (18), ADIφ ⩾1 (φ is t or f), and when each ridge point uniquely corresponds to
a coordinate point in a certain direction, ADIφ = 1, and when multiple ridge points correspond to the same coordinate in a certain
direction, ADIφ is much greater than 1.
To sum up, the specific steps of the mode-type discrimination criterion are as follows
Step 1: Extract the ridges along time and frequency from the TFD of a mode (obtained from the previous section);
Step 2: Calculate ADIf of the ridge along time and ADIt of the ridge along frequency by (18), respectively;
Step 3: Classify the mode by the following criterion:
(1) If ADIf > 1, the mode is a chirp mode;
(2) If ADIt > 1, the mode is a dispersive mode;
(3) If ADIf = 1 and ADIt = 1, the mode is classified by comparing the slopes SR and constant 1. Specifically, when SR⩽1, it is a
chirp mode, and when SR > 1, it is a dispersive mode.

3.3. Algorithmic framework and implementation

4.2. Real-life applications
In this subsection, we apply the VGNDM to two experimental signals analyses to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed
method in real-life applications. Firstly, a real bat echolocation signal [39] is considered and its time-domain waveform and TFD by
STFT are shown in Fig. 18. It can only be observed from Fig. 18(b) that the signal contains four modes with different time supports and
modulation laws, but it is difficult to obtain detailed information about these modes, such as support intervals and types of these
modes. Limited to these unknown information, there are few methods to effectively separate these signal modes and obtain accurate
time supports.
The VGNMD is applied to analyze the bat echolocation signal and the results are shown in Fig. 19. It can be seen that the final fused
TFD contains only four modes with almost no noise (see Fig. 19(a)), and the TF coefficients of the four modes are also precisely
clustered (see Fig. 19(b)). According to the clustering results of the four modes, their time supports can be obtained accurately. Based
on the clustering results, the mode-type discrimination criterion is applied to identify types of these four modes and all modes are
classified as chirp modes. Driven by the TF ridges of these modes as the initial IFs (see Fig. 19(c)), the variational optimization al­
gorithm extracts the four modes (see Fig. 19(d)) and further refines the corresponding IFs (see Fig. 19(c)) in their time intervals. The
extracted modes in Fig. 19(d) clearly show that the time of arrival (TOA) of these signal modes is different. Specifically, m1 arrives at
first, followed by m2 and m3, and m4 is the last one to arrive. The bat relies on the TOA properties to locate food and sense the
surroundings.
Fig. 20 shows the TFDs by ACMD, GDMD, SST, and VGNMD, respectively. The ACMD and GDMD only extract the first four modes.
As you can see, neither ACMD nor GDMD can correctly extract these four modes because they do not persist in the reference interval.
Limited by moving the TF coefficients along time direction, the SST can not effectively improve the concentration of dispersion
components. Furthermore, it is difficult to accurately show the TOA of the modes. Compared with the above methods, the VGNMD
successfully separates these modes and precisely obtains the duration and effective bandwidths of each mode. Herein the cost times of
the ACMD, GDMD, SST and VGNMD are 0.23 s, 0.32 s, 0.12 s and 0.34 s, respectively.

5. Conclusion
In this paper, a novel signal decomposition framework named VGNMD is proposed to analyze the GNSs with chirp modes and
dispersive modes. The VGNMD framework first obtains the high-quality TFD of the signal and modes using an ATFFC scheme. Then,
based on the TFDs of these modes, a mode-type discrimination criterion is employed to classify modes into chirp modes or dispersive
modes. At the same time, the durations and effective bandwidths of these modes are obtained according to the nonzero TF values in
their TFDs. Finally, driven by TF ridges of these modes, the variational optimization algorithm accurately estimates the modes and
their IFs or GDs from the original signal in corresponding durations and effective bandwidths. Simulated examples and the comparison
results with the state-of-art methods show that the VGNMD can simultaneously extract chirp modes and dispersive modes in the GNS
and generate a high-quality TFD. Then, real-life applications in bat echolocation signal analysis and railway wheel/rail fault diagnosis
have also been carried out, demonstrating that the VGNMD can characterize chirp signals and transient impulse signals and is wellsuitable for analyzing nonlinear signals with discontinuous TF patterns.
We should point out that when there are only the chirp modes or dispersive modes in the generalized nonlinear signal, it is reduced
to a nonlinear chirp signal or a nonlinear dispersive signal. Therefore, the VGNMD can also applicable for nonlinear chirp signals and
nonlinear dispersive signals. It is worth noting that the VGNMD is powerless when the IFs or GDs of signal modes are so close to or cross
in the time–frequency domain that it does not satisfy the qualification condition of the generalized nonlinear signal.
