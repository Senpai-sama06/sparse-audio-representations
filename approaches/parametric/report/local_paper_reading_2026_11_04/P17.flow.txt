ABSTRACT
Variational mode decomposition (VMD) and its extensions like Multivariate VMD (MVMD) decompose
signals into ensembles of band-limited modes with narrow central frequencies using Fourier transformations.
However, since these transformations span the entire time-domain signal, they are suboptimal for analyzing
non-stationary time series.
We introduce Short-Time Variational Mode Decomposition (STVMD), an innovative extension of VMD
that incorporates Short-Time Fourier transform (STFT) to minimize the impact of local disturbances. STVMD
segments signals into short time windows and converts these segments into the frequency domain. It then
formulates a variational optimization problem to extract band-limited modes representing the windowed data.
The optimization aims to minimize the sum of mode bandwidths across the windowed data, extending the cost
functions used in VMD and MVMD. Solutions are derived using the alternating direction method of multipliers,
ensuring extraction of modes with narrow bandwidths.
STVMD is divided into dynamic and non-dynamic types, depending on whether central frequencies vary
with time. Our experiments show non-dynamic STVMD matches VMD with properly sized time windows,
while dynamic STVMD better accommodates non-stationary signals through reduced mode function errors and
tracking of dynamic frequencies. This effectiveness is validated using steady-state visual-evoked potentials in
electroencephalogram signals.

1. Introduction
Non-stationary signals, characterized by time-varying statistical
properties like frequency, are prevalent in fields like biomedical engineering, telecommunications, and environmental monitoring. Signal
processing methods on non-stationary signals have attracted a lot of
interests due to its wide applications in the real-world. Here we focus
on tracking the time variations of packets of close frequencies in input
data, i.e., decomposing the input data into multiple packets with fewer
overlapped frequency bands.
Empirical mode decomposition (EMD) adaptively decomposes signals into intrinsic mode functions (IMFs) through a recursive sifting
process based on local extrema [1]. While EMD-based methods adapt
to local signal characteristics without predefined functions, they suffer
from mode mixing and lack theoretical guarantees [2].

Variational mode decomposition (VMD) addresses these limitations
by formulating decomposition as a constrained variational problem [3].
VMD decomposes signals into narrow-band mode functions around
central frequencies by minimizing collective bandwidth while ensuring signal reconstruction. The optimization is solved using the alternating direction method of multipliers. Recent work has expanded
VMD’s capabilities through variational time-frequency adaptive decomposition [4], improved spectrum reconstruction techniques [5],
and adaptive parameter selection methods [6], along with generalized
nonlinear mode decomposition [7] and efficient algorithms for specific
applications [8].
While VMD handles univariate signals, multivariate variational
mode decomposition (MVMD) extends VMD to jointly decompose multiple correlated signals [9]. Recent developments include grouped

2. Variational mode decomposition

MVMD [10], adaptive multivariate chirp mode decomposition [11],
and optimized algorithms for biomedical applications [12]. However,
VMD and its variants face fundamental limitations for non-stationary
signals with time-varying frequencies: (1) Global frequency domain
analysis - using Fourier transforms over entire signal duration prevents
localized frequency tracking; (2) Fixed central frequencies - constant
frequency assumptions fail to capture dynamic frequency evolution;
(3) Lack of temporal resolution - inability to analyze time-localized
frequency components; (4) Mode separation challenges - difficulties with
overlapping frequencies that vary temporally [13,14].
In the EMD-based decomposition method, the decomposition process is related to the local extremes, and central frequencies of decomposed mode functions are not expressed as a numerical value during the
sifting process. In the VMD-based decomposition method, the central
frequencies are estimated and the mode functions share the same
central frequencies as time changes. Some extensions of VMD and EMD
improved the decomposition performance, such as the noise-assisted
EMD [15], noise-assisted VMD [16], or ensemble EMD [17]. Target at
solving the limitation of predefined number of mode functions, Successive VMD is proposed [18]. Some extensions, like grouped MVMD, are
proposed to solve the real-world applications [10].
In the stationary signal, the central frequencies of signals are fixed
and it is not necessary to track the dynamic changes of the central frequencies. Therefore, the EMD-based and VMD-based methods
ignore the information provided by the central frequencies. In the nonstationary signals, the frequencies are usually time-varying. Tracking
the dynamic changes of the central frequencies provides the decomposed methods more information.
Recent work by Zheng et al. [19] introduced initial concepts for
combining VMD with STFT analysis and suggested the possibility of
time-varying frequencies by analyzing windowed data through sliding operations. Another approach by Zhou et al. [20] used VMD to
extract components iteratively on windowed signals. However, these
works did not establish theoretical foundations connecting VMD to
time-frequency analysis or systematically address the aforementioned
limitations of global frequency domain constraints and fixed central
frequency assumptions. Our work addresses this fundamental gap by
developing Short-Time Variational Mode Decomposition (STVMD) that
directly tackles these challenges through temporal localization and
dynamic frequency tracking capabilities. We establish rigorous connections between traditional VMD and non-dynamic STVMD, then develop
dynamic STVMD for time-varying frequency tracking, supported by
extensive experimental validation demonstrating superior performance
in non-stationary signal decomposition.
The proposed STVMD model is developed by replacing the Fourier
transform with the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) in VMD, avoiding the negative influence of signal changes in the global time domain.
This approach complements existing VMD improvements that focus
on mode separation challenges [13,14] by specifically addressing the
temporal localization needed for non-stationary signal analysis. In the
STVMD model, we seek a collection of common modulated short-time
mode functions residing in the input signals that exhibit a minimum
collective bandwidth while fully reconstructing the input signals. The
key innovation of the proposed method is summarized in the following
three points:
(1) The windowed variational decomposition replaces global
Fourier transform with Short-Time Fourier Transform to enable localized frequency analysis;
(2) The time-varying central frequency allows frequency centers
to adapt dynamically across time windows for non-stationary signal
tracking;
(3) A generalized VMD framework unifies stationary and nonstationary signal decomposition where traditional VMD becomes a
special case.
The paper is organized as follows. Sections 2 and 3 review the
basic concepts for VMD and STFT, respectively. Section 4 explains
the non-dynamic STVMD and dynamic STVMD in detail. Section 5
systematically analyzes the relationship between VMD, non-dynamic
STVMD and dynamic STVMD.

Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) is a sophisticated signal
processing technique designed to decompose a given signal into a set of
modes, each with a specific central frequency and narrow bandwidth.
Providing a robust and mathematically principled framework, VMD
can be divided into several key components: intrinsic mode functions,
Hilbert transform, and the variational decomposition process itself.

Offline process refers to data processing on the collected and stored
data. Online processing refers to real-time processing of incoming data
signals, which involves analyzing and manipulating the data as it is
collected, without the need to store the entire data set before starting
the processing task.
In VMD and MVMD, the entire time series are fed into the Fourier
transform for the decomposition of signal in the Fourier domain. This
operation indicates that VMD and MVMD are offline signal processing
algorithms, and suffer from heavy computation load in the online
process because of the computation of the whole dataset.
In the dynamic STVMD, the central frequencies are independent for
two non-adjacent time windows. In the online process, new signals are
added to the original signals. The newly added signals can influence the
central frequencies of all signals, so it is necessary to update the central
frequencies. In dynamic STVMD, due to the use of short time windows,
the influence of new signals is limited to the latest 𝑁 sampling points,
where 𝑁 is the length of the time window. The pseudo computation
process is given in Algorithm 4. In the offline process, before applying
STVMD, the reflection padding on both left and right sides is used to
apply the window function on the signals. But in the online process,
the right-side reflection padding is used because the cached signals are
used on the left side.

In this section, experiments and simulations are used to evaluate the
performance of the proposed STVMD method, and the detailed results
are given.
The experiments include two points: (1) Comparison between VMD
(or MVMD) and non-dynamic STVMD, to analyze the influence induced
by the short time windows; (2) New property of decomposed signals in
the dynamic STVMD compared to non-dynamic STVMD. The default
parameter setting for both VMD (or MVMD) and STVMD is given in
Table 1.

literature and preliminary testing, providing a good balance between
mode bandwidth minimization and reconstruction fidelity. The number
of modes 𝐾 was adapted based on signal characteristics: for synthetic
signals, 𝐾 matched the expected number of frequency components; for
real EEG signals, 𝐾 was determined through spectral analysis of the
signal’s frequency content.

5.3. Real application scene
Electroencephalogram (EEG) signal is a biosignal acquired from the
scalp of human brain. The steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP)
is a brain activity which can be analyzed with EEG signals acquired
from visual cortex [23]. SSVEP refers to the brain signal evoked by
external stimulus with certain frequencies. For example, when the
subject views a stimulus at a frequency of 10 Hz, the EEG signal in
the visual cortex exhibits corresponding frequencies at 10 Hz and its
harmonics, such as 20 Hz and 30 Hz, with decreased power.
In this experiment, the dynamic STVMD is applied to EEG signals
in channel 𝑂𝑧 , where 𝑂𝑧 is the electrode located in the visual cortex
in the international 10–20 system. The signal is a 10-s time series with
sampling rate 250 Hz. The first five seconds and the last five seconds
of the EEG signals are acquired when the subject is stimulated with
10 Hz and 13 Hz stimuli, respectively. The responses to 10 Hz in the
visual cortex are at 10 Hz and it harmonics like 20 Hz and 30 Hz. The
responses to 13 Hz in the visual cortex are at 13 Hz and it harmonics
like 26 Hz and 39 Hz.
To remove the influence of noise, the signals are averaged from six
epochs and then normalized. In the frequency domain, the power of
signals in the low-frequency bands exhibits a decreasing trend. We first
average the time–frequency map across the time dimension and obtain
the power changes in the frequency domain. The trend is estimated
using the five adjacent values (two to the left, two to the right and the
same central value). To eliminate the influence of the trend, the trend is
removed from the time–frequency map in STVMD. Taking into account
that the power decreases with increasing frequencies, we use two
components of the responses so that the input signals for STVMD are
bandpassed between 5 Hz and 30 Hz. The decomposition comparison
between dynamic STVMD and non-dynamic STVMD are shown in Fig.
11. The comparison of mode functions between non-dynamic STVMD,
VMD and EMD is given in Fig. 12.
Dynamic STVMD introduces significantly higher computational
overhead compared to VMD and non-dynamic STVMD. The method
requires a complete decomposition loop for every time window, with
computational complexity scaling as O (𝑇 × 𝐾 × iterations), where
𝑇 is the number of time windows. In our EEG experiments, dynamic
STVMD required much longer processing time than VMD for the same
10-s signal, limiting its applicability for real-time processing.
The window length 𝑁 represents a critical trade-off between temporal and frequency resolution. For SSVEP signals, we empirically selected
𝑁 = 250 (1 s) based on expected frequency transition characteristics,
but this choice may not generalize to other biomedical applications.

The application to real EEG signals reveals practical benefits of
dynamic STVMD in biomedical signal processing. The method successfully tracked frequency transitions in steady-state visual-evoked
potentials, demonstrating its utility in analyzing brain signals where
frequency components evolve over time. This capability has significant
implications for brain-computer interface applications and neurological
diagnostics.
However, our analysis also reveals important limitations. The computational complexity increases with the number of mode functions,
limiting real-time applications. Additionally, the trade-off between time
and frequency resolution in STFT carries over to STVMD, requiring
parameter selection based on application requirements. Small time
windows lead to poor frequency resolution, while large windows miss
rapid frequency changes and introduce latency in online processing.
Looking ahead, several promising research directions emerge. First,
adaptive parameter selection mechanisms could optimize the window
length and number of modes based on signal characteristics. Second, the integration of machine learning techniques might enable
more efficient computation and automated parameter tuning. Finally,
the theoretical framework of STVMD could be extended to handle
multivariate signals with more complex interchannel relationships.
