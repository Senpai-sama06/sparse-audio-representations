Density-Based Adaptive Mode Decomposition
Hao Jia , Chunyu Yang , Cesar F. Caiafa , Zhe Sun , Feng Duan , and Jordi Solé-Casals

Abstract—Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) requires
manual specification of mode numbers and center frequencies,
limiting its practical applicability for non-stationary signals.
This paper introduces Density-based Adaptive Mode Decomposition (DAMD), which addresses these limitations through three
key innovations. First, we establish mathematical equivalence
between VMD optimization and density-based clustering, enabling automatic mode detection through meanshift clustering
with adaptive bandwidth estimation. Second, we develop Direct
Mode Extraction that replaces iterative optimization with efficient clustering, and Hybrid Variational Refinement for selective precision enhancement. Third, we extend the framework
to diverse time-frequency representations including wavelets
and synchrosqueezed transforms. Experimental validation across
biomedical signals, gravitational waves, mechanical faults, and
speech data demonstrates significant computational improvements while maintaining perfect reconstruction. DAMD eliminates manual parameter tuning and enables automated adaptive
signal decomposition for complex non-stationary signals.
Index Terms—Signal processing, mode decomposition, timefrequency analysis, adaptive clustering, non-stationary signals.

I. INTRODUCTION

T

IME-FREQUENCY analysis is fundamental for understanding non-stationary signals across diverse domains
including biomedical engineering, telecommunications and so
on. While traditional Fourier analysis excels at stationary signal
analysis [1], it fails to capture the dynamic characteristics of
real-world signals where frequency content evolves over time.
This limitation has driven significant developments in adaptive
signal processing, with mode decomposition emerging as a
powerful framework for analyzing non-stationary phenomena.
The challenge of non-stationary signal analysis lies in automatically identifying and extracting meaningful mode functions
while adapting to temporal variations in signal complexity. Traditional approaches often require manual parameter tuning, limiting their practical utility in automated systems. For instance, in
real-time health monitoring systems, signal characteristics can
change rapidly with patient conditions, requiring continuous
adaptation of processing parameters that cannot be achieved
through manual tuning.
Mode decomposition methods have evolved significantly
since the introduction of Empirical Mode Decomposition
(EMD) [2]. While EMD pioneered adaptive signal decomposition through intrinsic mode functions (IMFs), its empirical
nature leads to mode mixing and lacks theoretical foundations.
Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) [3] and its multichannel version Multivariate VMD (MVMD) [4] addressed
these limitations by reformulating mode extraction as a variational optimization problem. However, VMD’s requirement
for pre-specified mode numbers and manual parameter tuning
remains a significant constraint in practical applications.
Recent methodological developments have addressed VMD
limitations through three principal research directions, each
assuming predetermined mode numbers while contributing theoretical innovations to decomposition quality. Spectrum-based
enhancement methods improve spectral analysis capabilities
through mathematical reformulations, with Liu et al. [5] developing generalized demodulation algorithms for overlapped
mode components. Parameter optimization strategies advance
systematic frameworks for balancing and convergence parameters, as demonstrated by Chen et al. [6] through improved
whale optimization algorithms for penalty factor selection and
Yu et al. [7] via sample entropy-based adaptive parameter tuning. Theoretical framework extensions expand mathematical
foundations through advanced variational formulations, including Wang et al.’s [8] variational generalized nonlinear mode
decomposition for chirp and dispersive components and Jian
et al.’s [9] de-mixing formulations to reduce mode mixing

artifacts. While these advances demonstrate significant progress
in decomposition accuracy, noise robustness, and mathematical
rigor, they fundamentally rely on prior knowledge of mode
numbers, creating a critical bottleneck for practical applications
where signal complexity is unknown.
To address this fundamental limitation, automatic mode number determination methods have emerged as a critical research
frontier, employing diverse strategies to eliminate the need for
prior mode specification. Successive decomposition approaches
extract modes iteratively without predefined limits, with Nazari
and Sakhaei [10] introducing successive VMD that adds spectral overlap criteria to extract modes sequentially, and Liu and
Yu [11] extending this to multivariate signals through successive multivariate VMD. Information-theoretic and statistical
metrics provide quantitative criteria for optimal mode detection,
including Lian et al.’s [12] mode characteristic-based adaptive
VMD, Gao et al.’s [13] impulse diversity entropy approach
using sparrow search algorithm, and Xu et al.’s [14] hybrid
entropy combining envelope fuzzy and dispersion characteristics. Clustering-based approaches leverage signal frequency
distribution patterns, with Gu et al. [15] employing improved
density peak clustering on time-frequency information to determine mode numbers automatically. Meta-heuristic optimization
frameworks systematically search optimal parameter combinations, with Wang et al. [16] employing Archimedes optimization algorithm with correlation waveform index and Yan
et al. [17] developing improved particle swarm optimization
for dynamic parameter determination. However, these automatic approaches face persistent challenges in computational
complexity and convergence stability. A critical limitation of
all aforementioned methods, including successive VMD and
clustering-based algorithms, is that they only address the K
selection problem while retaining the VMD iterative process,
resulting in substantial computational time consumption.
The computational efficiency challenge becomes even more
critical when considering recent time-localized VMD variants.
Short-time variational mode decomposition, as proposed by
Zheng et al. [18] and Jia et al. [19], performs VMD operations at
each time sampling point within sliding windows, dramatically
escalating computational burden. Jia et al.’s work distinguishes
between non-dynamic STVMD, where mode center frequencies
remain constant across windows, and dynamic STVMD, which
allows mode center frequencies to adapt across different time
windows for enhanced adaptability to non-stationary signals.
Similarly, recursive windowed VMD methods [20] apply VMD
decomposition at every temporal location, resulting in computational complexity that scales multiplicatively with signal
length.
These limitations create acute challenges for dynamic
STVMD applications, which must perform VMD optimization
at thousands of temporal locations while adaptively selecting K
values that vary across time windows in non-stationary signals.
This dual challenge necessitates novel methods that simultaneously achieve computational efficiency and real-time adaptive selection of the number of mode functions, representing a
fundamental shift from existing approaches that address these
issues separately.

1369

To address these challenges, we present Density-based Adaptive Mode Decomposition (DAMD), which advances the stateof-the-art through three key innovations. First, we establish a
theoretical connection between VMD and density-based clustering [21], enabling automatic, data-driven determination of
optimal mode numbers and initial frequencies through Meanshift clustering with bandwidth estimation, eliminating the need
for manual mode number specification. Second, we introduce
complementary mode extraction approaches: Direct Mode Extraction (DME) that replaces VMD’s computationally intensive iterative optimization with efficient data-driven Meanshift
clustering, achieving significant speed improvements, and Hybrid Variational Refinement (HVR) for cases requiring precise
separation. Third, we extend VMD beyond traditional Fourierbased spectrograms to support various time-frequency representations [22], broadening applicability to diverse signal processing scenarios.
This paper is organized as follows: Section II presents the theoretical foundations and its implementation; Section III demonstrates experimental results on synthetic and real-world signals;
Section IV discusses findings and comparisons with existing
methods; Section V concludes with key insights.
II. PROPOSED METHOD
This section introduces the DAMD framework, a comprehensive methodology that addresses fundamental limitations in
traditional mode decomposition approaches through theoretical
innovation and practical algorithmic development. The framework integrates three key methodological foundations: (1) a theoretical equivalence between VMD optimization and densitybased clustering that enables direct frequency domain partitioning, (2) Meanshift clustering for automatic mode number determination and center frequency detection, and (3) generalized
time-frequency representations that extend beyond traditional
Fourier analysis for non-stationary signal analysis.

IV. DISCUSSION
This work presents the DAMD framework for non-stationary
signal analysis, establishing a novel theoretical foundation that
bridges variational mode decomposition with density-based
clustering. The framework introduces fundamental innovations
that address critical limitations in traditional mode decomposition approaches. Three key aspects warrant detailed discussion
regarding the practical implications and theoretical contributions of this work.
A distinctive characteristic of DAMD lies in its frequency
domain decomposition approach without explicit time domain
reconstruction during the decomposition process. Traditional
VMD methods perform iterative ADMM optimization in the
frequency domain and ultimately require inverse transforms to
obtain time-domain mode functions. In contrast, our clusteringbased approach directly partitions the frequency domain without iterative refinement, and the resulting mode characterization remains entirely in the frequency domain representation. This design choice significantly reduces computational
overhead, particularly when dealing with the adaptive number
of modes that can vary substantially across time windows.
This efficiency is justified by two observations: first, DAMD’s

1379

modes exhibit predominantly sinusoidal characteristics that are
inherently well-represented in the frequency domain; second,
comprehensive mode characterization through time location
and center frequency information captures essential properties without requiring time-domain conversion. This approach
proves advantageous for applications where spectral characteristics and temporal evolution are more critical than exact
time-domain waveforms. Furthermore, with proper bandwidth
estimation and appropriate time-frequency representations, the
DME approach often provides sufficiently accurate mode characterization, making HVR refinement unnecessary for most
applications unless very high precision is specifically required.
From a signal interpretation perspective, DAMD should therefore be viewed as a principled frequency-domain partitioning
framework, whose physical interpretability depends on the underlying signal structure and the chosen time–frequency representation, rather than as a generative model of intrinsic timedomain oscillatory modes.
Traditional multi-channel variational mode decomposition
methods, such as Multivariate VMD (MVMD), decompose
all channels simultaneously under shared constraints including
common mode numbers and synchronized frequency characteristics. While ensuring cross-channel consistency, this approach may obscure channel-specific spectral features and impose unnecessary computational burden. DAMD enables independent decomposition of each channel, allowing channelspecific adaptation with optimal mode numbers and unique
frequency characteristics. This flexibility proves particularly
valuable in multi-electrode EEG analysis where different brain
regions exhibit distinct spectral signatures, and in multi-sensor
vibration monitoring where sensors capture varying fault signatures. The ability to apply different time-frequency representations or bandwidth estimation methods to different channels
further enhances adaptability while enabling efficient parallel
processing. However, multichannel applications often benefit
from domain-specific spatial optimization techniques that exploit cross-channel correlations, such as proper orthogonal decomposition in computational fluid dynamics, task-related component analysis in SSVEP-based brain-computer interfaces, and
common spatial pattern in motor imagery classification. Integrating DAMD’s temporal-spectral decomposition with these
spatial methods represents a promising future direction.
DAMD fundamentally shifts the parameter dependency
paradigm from opaque algorithmic tuning to interpretable
signal-related characteristics. Unlike traditional VMD methods that require manual specification of penalty factors and
predefined mode numbers (parameters with limited physical
interpretation), DAMD eliminates these algorithmic parameters while introducing more intuitive dependencies that directly
relate to signal characteristics. The framework’s performance
is primarily influenced by three key factors analyzed in Section III: time-frequency representation type (STFT, CWT, SSQSTFT, SSQ-CWT), time-frequency resolution controlled by
window length, and bandwidth estimation method determining
clustering sensitivity. The experimental analysis demonstrates
that these parameter choices produce predictable effects on
decomposition performance, enabling users to make informed

It is important to clarify DAMD’s role within the signal
processing pipeline. DAMD is a post-processing tool that performs frequency domain partitioning on pre-computed timefrequency representations; it does not perform denoising or
signal-noise separation. Noise robustness is therefore inherited
from the chosen time–frequency representation. The quality
of DAMD’s output is fundamentally bounded by the quality
of the input time-frequency map. If the underlying transform
cannot resolve signal components due to insufficient SNR,
spectral leakage, or inappropriate parameter selection, DAMD
will partition whatever spectral content is present, including
noise artifacts. However, the framework naturally provides
mode-level energy quantification through the mean squared
amplitude of extracted mode functions, enabling principled
post-hoc filtering. High-energy modes typically correspond to
physically meaningful signal components, while low-energy
modes often represent noise-dominated frequency partitions
required for complete coverage. This energy-based stratification offers practical value: users can apply domain-specific
thresholds to retain only modes exceeding certain energy percentiles, or weight downstream analyses by mode energy to
emphasize dominant components. Such energy-aware processing bridges DAMD’s exhaustive frequency partitioning with
application-specific requirements for mode significance assessment. Additionally, selecting time-frequency transforms with
better energy concentration (e.g., synchrosqueezed variants)
can improve the quality of the input representation before
applying DAMD.
The theoretical foundation established through the VMDclustering equivalence represents a significant advancement
in signal processing theory, providing mathematical rigor for
density-based approaches to mode decomposition. The proven
connection between variational optimization and clustering
algorithms opens new avenues for algorithm development
and theoretical analysis in adaptive signal processing. Future research directions include optimization of the selection criteria between DME and HVR approaches for specific signal characteristics, extension to other clustering algorithms beyond Meanshift, systematic investigation of multichannel integration strategies combining DAMD with domainspecific spatial optimization techniques, and exploration of applications in emerging domains such as time series analysis
for large language models. The potential for unified, distinguished, and recoverable embedding representations, analogous to developments in image processing and natural language processing, presents exciting opportunities for advancing
the field of time series analysis through the clustering-based
decomposition.
V. CONCLUSION
This paper presents DAMD, which transforms mode decomposition from manual parameter tuning to automated,
data-driven clustering. We establish mathematical equivalence
between VMD and density-based clustering, enabling automatic mode detection through meanshift algorithms. The
framework provides two approaches: DME for computational

efficiency and HVR for enhanced precision. Validation across
biomedical, gravitational wave, mechanical, and speech signals
demonstrates significant computational improvements while
maintaining perfect reconstruction. By eliminating manual parameter specification and extending to diverse time-frequency
representations, DAMD represents a significant advancement
toward fully automated adaptive signal decomposition for complex non-stationary signals.
